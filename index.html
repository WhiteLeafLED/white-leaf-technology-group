Technology · Wavelengths

Where LED light sits on the spectrum.

Light is a wave. Two simple measures tell you what kind of light it is.

Frequency

How often it repeats.

Frequency is how many wave cycles pass a point each second. Higher frequency means the wave oscillates faster.

Wavelength

How long each wave is.

Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next. A longer wavelength means the wave is more stretched out.

Higher frequencies have shorter wavelengths. Lower frequencies have longer wavelengths.

White Leaf’s red and blue LED fixtures sit in that visible band, each at its own wavelength and frequency.

Technology · What is an LED

A light emitting diode.

Light emitting diodes (LEDs) are semiconductors that convert electrical energy into light energy.

The color of the emitted light depends on the semiconductor material and its composition.

LEDs are generally classified into three wavelengths:

  • Ultraviolet
  • Visible
  • Infrared

Technology · Emerging technology

From NASA plant research to cell healing.

In 1993, Quantum Devices of Barneveld, WI developed a light-emitting diode for NASA to use in its plant growth experiments.

A NASA-sponsored research center at the University of Wisconsin-Madison developed Astro culture 3 — a plant growth chamber using near infrared HEALS technology for plant growth experiments on shuttle missions.

LEDs release energy in the form of photons. Their long wavelengths of light stimulate cells to aid in healing — and NASA went on to study LEDs to increase the metabolism of human cells and stem the loss of bone and muscle in astronauts.

NASA’s research extended from plants to people — using LED light to support human cells, bone and muscle on missions.

Human eyes

Light therapy used on human eyes.

LED light therapy is used and being researched for certain eye conditions. It works by boosting mitochondrial function in retinal cells and improving blood circulation, which can help slow disease progression and reduce inflammation.

  • Dry eye & MGD: red and near-infrared masks gently warm the eyelids, melting blockages so the glands can secrete natural oils and stabilize tears.
  • Dry AMD: systems like the Valeda Light Delivery System stimulate energy production in retinal cells, reducing drusen buildup and slowing vision loss.
  • Diabetic retinopathy: near-infrared wavelengths help protect photoreceptors and reduce inflammation-related damage (experimental and clinical).
  • Blepharitis: blue or red light is sometimes applied to reduce bacterial load and inflammation along the eyelids.

Hospitals

Blue light treating jaundice.

Blue light is used to treat jaundice in babies. Here’s how it works:

  • Bilirubin buildup. Jaundice happens when a baby’s immature liver cannot process bilirubin (a yellow pigment from broken-down red blood cells) fast enough, causing it to build up in the blood and turn the skin and eyes yellow.
  • Light absorption. The baby is placed under special therapeutic lights — typically in the blue or blue-green spectrum — wearing only a diaper and protective eye patches.
  • Photochemical change. As the blue light penetrates the skin, it triggers a photochemical reaction (photoisomerization). This changes the structure of the water-insoluble bilirubin into water-soluble isomers like lumirubin.

Applications/Results · The Unseen Enemy

The Unseen Enemy

Several key bacteria threaten dairy farm milking operations, causing udder infections, milk quality degradation, and animal health risks.

Mastitis is the most costly disease in dairy farming, damaging udder tissue and reducing milk yield.

Key pathogens

  • Staphylococcus aureus (Contagious) — Spreads primarily during the milking process and causes chronic, subclinical infections that elevate somatic cell counts (SCC).
  • Streptococcus agalactiae (Contagious) — Lives strictly inside the bovine udder and historically caused severe clinical mastitis and high cell counts.
  • Escherichia coli (E. coli) (Environmental) — Originates from manure and organic bedding, entering the teat canal to cause acute, severe, and sometimes fatal toxic mastitis.
  • Streptococcus uberis (Environmental) — Found in pasture, manure, and bedding; responsible for a large share of both clinical and subclinical mastitis cases.
  • Streptococcus dysgalactiae (Environmental/Contagious) — Inhabits teat skin and mucous membranes, spreading easily if post-milking teat dips are missed.
  • Klebsiella (Environmental) — Often linked to wood-based, sawdust, or sand bedding; causes severe clinical mastitis that frequently leads to chronic infection and culling.

Does fighting bacteria take a lot of energy?

Fighting off bacteria takes an immense amount of energy from a milking cow, particularly in the form of glucose.

When a dairy cow’s immune system detects bad bacteria—whether in her environment, on her feed, or in her gut—it triggers an inflammatory response. Research from groups like the Wageningen Livestock Research Institute shows that a fully activated immune system drastically shifts the cow’s metabolic priorities.

The cost of defense · “The Glucose Thief”

  • Massive energy consumption — An acute immune response can consume over 1 kilogram (2.2 lbs) of pure glucose every 12 hours.
  • Prioritization over milk — Milking cows need glucose to make lactose (milk sugar). However, the immune system takes complete priority. When white blood cells are fighting pathogens, they “steal” that glucose, increasing the cow’s baseline maintenance energy requirements by up to 40%.
  • Severe production drops — A major bacterial challenge can cause a sudden drop in milk production of 20 to 40 pounds or more per day because the energy is diverted away from the udder.

Where the enemy bacteria hide

Cows constantly balance their energy to fight off bacteria across three main areas:

  1. The gut & feed (mycotoxins and pathogens) — Mold, wild yeasts, or harmful bacteria like E. coli and Salmonella in poor-quality feed can damage the gut lining. This causes a “leaky gut,” forcing the cow’s immune system to spend vast resources defending her bloodstream from leaking toxins.
  2. The environment (bedding and mud) — Environmental bacteria (like Streptococcus uberis) are constantly trying to enter the teat canals when the cow lies down. Preventing or battling environmental mastitis is one of the heaviest energy drains a dairy cow can face.
  3. The rumen balance — A cow relies on billions of good microbes in her rumen to ferment feed into energy. If bad bacteria or sudden feed changes disrupt this balance, the rumen becomes less efficient, leaving the cow with less overall energy to live, produce milk, or stay healthy.

Because a high-producing dairy cow operates on a very tight energy budget, even a minor, invisible infection forces her to pull energy from her own body fat and muscle, which often triggers secondary metabolic issues like ketosis.
